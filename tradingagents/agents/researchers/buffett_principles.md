# How Warren Buffett Evaluates a Business

## Circle of Competence

The boundary matters more than the size. "What counts for most people in investing is
not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know."
A business qualifies only if you can form a confident view of what it will look like in
ten years: who its customers are, why they keep coming back, what could take them away,
and roughly what it will earn. Complexity, rapid technological change, and dependence on
regulatory or political outcomes push a company outside the circle regardless of how
attractive the numbers look.

The test is not whether an industry is important, but whether its long-run economics are
predictable. Many transformative industries have been terrible for owners, because the
benefit flowed to customers and competitors rather than shareholders.

## Economic Moats and Franchise Quality

Think of a business as "an economic castle protected by an unbreachable moat." The
central question is what protects returns from the competitors that high returns
inevitably attract, and whether that protection is widening or narrowing.

A true franchise has a product or service that is needed or desired, has no close
substitute, and is not price-regulated. The practical signature is pricing power: the
ability to raise prices regularly without losing units to a competitor or suffering
material volume decline. A franchise can survive mediocre management; a commodity
business cannot. In a commodity business, only the low-cost producer earns a durable
return, and even that advantage is usually thin.

Sources of a moat worth crediting: entrenched brand and habit, low-cost production at
scale, switching costs, network effects, and regulatory or locational advantage. Sources
that do not count: a good current product without repeat economics, a temporary
technology lead, a strong recent growth rate, or a large addressable market.

Ask which way the moat is trending. A widening moat justifies patience; a narrowing one
means the terminal value is an illusion no matter how good this year's earnings are.

## Owner Earnings and Intrinsic Value

Intrinsic value is "the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business
during its remaining life." It is an estimate, not a computation, and it changes as
interest rates and business prospects change. Two honest analysts will get different
numbers; that is expected.

Reported earnings are the starting point, not the answer. Owner earnings are reported
earnings plus depreciation, amortization, and other non-cash charges, minus the average
annual capital expenditure the business genuinely requires to maintain its competitive
position and unit volume. That last term is a judgment call, which is precisely why the
figure is more useful than the one on the income statement.

Treat EBITDA as a warning, not a metric: depreciation is a real expense representing
cash already spent, and maintenance capex is a real claim on future cash. Ask whether
management believes the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures.

For a partially owned position, remember that retained earnings that are wisely deployed
still accrue to owners even when no dividend arrives. Look-through earnings, the share
of investees' retained earnings attributable to the holding, are part of the return.

## Margin of Safety and Price Discipline

Price and value are separate questions. A wonderful business bought at too high a price
is a poor investment; the quality of the asset does not rescue the arithmetic.

Buy at a discount to a conservatively estimated intrinsic value, and make the estimate
conservative rather than the discount large. The margin of safety compensates for
estimation error, not for a known defect.

Buffett's thinking here visibly evolved. The early approach was Graham's cigar butt:
buy statistically cheap assets, take the last free puff, repeat. He explicitly repudiated
it as the capital base grew, concluding that "it's far better to buy a wonderful company
at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." The reason is compounding:
"time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." A mediocre
business bought cheaply requires a second correct decision, the exit, and usually
disappoints while you wait.

Related lessons paid for with real money: turnarounds seldom turn, and "good jockeys will
do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags." Do not underwrite a thesis that
depends on management fixing bad economics.

## Management: Candor and Capital Allocation

Two tests, applied to the record rather than the presentation.

Candor: does management report results the way it would want to be reported to if the
positions were reversed? Look for owning up to mistakes by name, disclosing the metrics
that matter even when unflattering, and avoiding the ritual of always hitting the
guided number. Managers who always promise to make the numbers will eventually be
tempted to make up the numbers.

Capital allocation: it is the single most consequential thing a CEO does, and most
arrive in the job with no training in it. Apply the one-dollar test to retained earnings:
over a sustained period, has each dollar retained created at least a dollar of market
value? Judge acquisitions on price paid against value received, not on the growth in
reported size. Judge buybacks on whether they were made below a conservative estimate of
intrinsic value; repurchases above it destroy value for continuing owners.

Watch for the institutional imperative: the tendency of organizations to imitate peers,
resist changing direction, generate supporting analysis for whatever the leader already
wants, and expand for its own sake.

## Return on Unleveraged Equity

"The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high
earnings rate on equity capital employed" without undue leverage or accounting
gimmickry. Earnings per share growth is a weak signal, because retained earnings alone
produce it: a savings account with reinvested interest shows rising annual earnings and
tells you nothing about management skill.

Growth creates value only when incremental capital earns more than its cost. Sort the
business into one of three buckets:

- Great: high returns on tangible capital with little reinvestment required, so growth is
  nearly free and cash comes out.
- Good: high returns, but growth consumes proportional capital, so the owner funds the
  expansion.
- Gruesome: rapid growth, heavy capital appetite, and little or no return on that
  capital. The worst combination, and one that often looks exciting from the outside.

Leverage deserves particular suspicion. Borrowed money has made some people rich and many
people poor, and a high return on equity manufactured with debt is not evidence of a good
business. Good businesses generally do not need much of it.

## Accounting Red Flags

Treat these as reasons to look harder, and sometimes as reasons to walk away:

- Recurring "one-time," restructuring, or non-recurring charges. If they recur, they are
  operating expenses.
- Prominence given to pro-forma, adjusted, or EBITDA figures over GAAP earnings and cash
  flow.
- Aggressive or opaque revenue recognition, and receivables or inventory growing faster
  than sales.
- Optimistic pension return or discount-rate assumptions that flatter earnings.
- Serial acquisitions that make organic performance impossible to isolate, and
  acquisition accounting that buries costs on the balance sheet.
- A widening gap between reported net income and cash from operations.
- Compensation structures that reward size, share price over short windows, or metrics
  management itself defines.
- Economic goodwill and accounting goodwill diverging: the amortization or impairment
  schedule usually says more about the price paid than about the asset's real worth.

## Temperament and Mr. Market

The market exists to serve you, not to instruct you. Imagine a manic-depressive partner
who quotes you a price every day; some days he is euphoric, some days despondent. You are
free to transact at his price or ignore him entirely, and he will be back tomorrow
regardless. A quoted price is an opportunity, never a verdict on value.

Volatility is not risk. Risk is the permanent loss of purchasing power, which comes from
overpaying, from a deteriorating business, or from being forced to sell at the wrong time.

Be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful. Inactivity is a
legitimate and usually superior position: lethargy bordering on sloth is a feature of the
approach, not a failure of it. Imagine a punch card with twenty slots for a lifetime of
decisions; the constraint improves the decisions. And if you would not be willing to own
the business for ten years, do not own it for ten minutes.

## The Too-Hard Pile

Declining to have a view is a legitimate and frequently correct outcome. There is no
penalty for passing, and no requirement to swing at every pitch.

Route a company to the too-hard pile when: the ten-year competitive picture is genuinely
unforecastable; the outcome depends on technology transitions, commodity prices,
regulatory rulings, or litigation you cannot handicap; the accounting cannot be
reconciled to cash; the business is a black box such as a large financial institution's
trading book; leverage makes the equity a levered option on an uncertain asset; or the
asset generates no cash and is worth only what the next buyer will pay.

Saying "I don't know" is a real answer and is better than a confident one built on
guesses. A missed opportunity costs nothing; a misjudged one costs capital.

## Evaluation Checklist

1. Do I understand how this business makes money, and can I describe its position ten
   years out? If not, stop here and say so.
2. What is the moat, concretely, and is it widening or narrowing? What evidence in the
   financials confirms it?
3. Does the company have pricing power? Has it raised prices without losing volume?
4. What are owner earnings, as distinct from reported earnings? What maintenance capex
   does the business truly require?
5. What is the return on equity and on tangible capital, excluding the effect of
   leverage? Has it been stable across a full cycle?
6. Is growth creating value, or consuming capital at returns below its cost? Which of the
   three buckets is this?
7. Has management been candid about mistakes, and does the record of acquisitions,
   buybacks, and retained earnings pass the one-dollar test?
8. Is the balance sheet conservative enough that a bad year is an inconvenience rather
   than an existential event?
9. What is a conservative estimate of intrinsic value, and what discount to it does
   today's price offer?
10. What would have to be true for this to be a permanent loss of capital, and how likely
    is that?
11. Given all of the above, is this a wonderful business at a fair price, a fair business,
    something outside the circle of competence, or simply too expensive today?
